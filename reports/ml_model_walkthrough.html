1

One model instead of ten

The old approach built a separate model per market. This model is pooled: every market shares one XGBoost model, and market_code (hits, total bases, home runs, spreads, and so on) is simply another input feature — not a reason to split the model.

The idea: a batter's recent form matters whether you're pricing his hits prop or his total-bases prop. A pooled model can share what it learns about form, matchups, and park effects across every market at once, instead of relearning it ten separate times on ten smaller slices of data. The model decides for itself, from the data, how much market type should shift a prediction — it isn't told the markets are different, it has to notice.

3

The split: per year, then pooled

Exactly as specified — not one global cutoff. Each calendar year is split 75/25 in chronological order on its own, then all four years' 75% are combined into one training set and all four years' 25% into one held-out test set.

2023
2024
2025
train (first 75% of each year, by date) test (final 25% of each year, by date)

The model never trains on a date it's later tested on, and every year contributes to both sides — so a good or bad 2024 alone can't dominate the result. All four years' test slices are then pooled into one number, which is what's reported throughout this page.

Three mistakes, all caught before they stood

Model B produced a headline number worth revising three times — and each time, the issue was in what went into the model or how it was trained, not a finding to protect. All three are shown here rather than quietly folded away.

Mistake 1 — alt-line row multiplicity

First run — killed before reporting
+46.63%
ROI, TEST holdout · AUC 0.857 · n=2,499
↓
Corrected
+4.98%
ROI, TEST holdout · AUC 0.764 · CI [1.13%, 8.82%]

What was wrong: player-prop lines are offered as a full ladder of alternates for every real game — a total-bases prop at 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, and 4.5, all at once, for the same player in the same game. The first version counted every alt-line as its own independent bet (917,981 rows collapsed to just 146,740 real player-games once corrected — a 6× inflation), and a naive fix picked the wrong line as "the" bet 90% of the time. Fixed: one row per real bet, keyed to the line whose price sits closest to a true coin flip.

Mistake 2 — a market added back in for the wrong reason, then re-excluded for the right one

pitcher_outs had been excluded on the stated grounds of "zero warehouse rows." That specific claim was false — it has 6,048 real rows, 2024-04-02 to 2025-05-28. Adding it back in looked like the correct fix and briefly reversed the whole result (down to ROI +0.01%, a FAIL) — but re-checking against the client's own harness config (market_config.ts) settled it properly: `pitcher_outs` is explicitly marked warehouseOddsAvailable: false, allowedSources: PICK_HISTORY_ONLY there — the identical treatment given to batter_runs_scored. That's a deliberate decision by the people who own this data (their official warehouse odds source apparently doesn't reliably carry this market, even though the xlsx export behind this project's local database happens to have some, for a narrow window). Re-excluded on that basis — back to 8 markets, for the correct reason this time.

Mistake 3 — treating any single run as "the" result

Rerunning the identical, corrected 8-market pipeline nine more times — fixed seed, single-threaded, explicit deterministic tiebreakers added to every sort/dedup step DuckDB's unordered row return could otherwise leave ambiguous — still produced a different edge>0.03 result nearly every time:

Five reruns, three different verdicts. After ruling out data-ordering ambiguity, the remaining source is XGBoost's own histogram-building, which isn't bit-for-bit deterministic in this environment even with a fixed seed and a single thread — a known category of floating-point non-determinism in gradient-boosted tree libraries, not a bug specific to this project.

6

The honest current result: marginal, not a confident PASS or FAIL

Given the instability above, no single run's number should be reported as settled. Here's the honest characterization instead.

ROI across reruns clusters roughly +1% to +8%, consistently on the positive side of zero on average — but the confidence interval crosses zero often enough (2 of 5 reruns shown above) that a single training run's verdict isn't trustworthy on its own. This is a genuinely different situation from every other market in this report: it isn't cleanly negative (like most markets), and it isn't a settled positive edge (like the hits odds-band finding) — it's a real signal that sits close enough to the noise floor of this model's own training process that the PASS/FAIL line depends on which run you happen to look at.

Practical takeaway: any future work on this specific model should train and report across multiple seeds/reruns as standard practice, not a single fit — and until that's done, this cut should be treated as marginal, not as a validated PASS to build on or a settled FAIL to discard. Every other finding in this report (the hits odds-band PASS, Model A and B's real-but-unprofitable ranking skill, every negative market) is unaffected by this — this instability is specific to this one pooled edge>0.03 cut.

7

Why "more accurate" isn't automatically "more profitable"

A real, unfabricated way to push accuracy above 80% exists in this data. It also loses money — which is exactly why it isn't what's reported as the model's headline number.

Betting whichever side the market already favors on batter_home_runs alone — no model required — wins 88.4% of the time, since most players simply don't hit a home run in a given game. ROI on that identical bet: −2.20%. The payout on such a heavy favorite is too small to cover the rare miss. Leaning a model toward that market would raise its accuracy number and lower its profitability at the same time — the two aren't the same target here, and this argument holds regardless of what Model B's own betting-edge result shows (see the correction above): an 80%+ accuracy figure built by leaning into home_runs would still lose money either way.
