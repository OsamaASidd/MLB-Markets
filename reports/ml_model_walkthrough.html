1

One model instead of ten

The old approach built a separate model per market. This model is pooled: every market shares one XGBoost model, and market_code (hits, total bases, home runs, spreads, and so on) is simply another input feature — not a reason to split the model.

The idea: a batter's recent form matters whether you're pricing his hits prop or his total-bases prop. A pooled model can share what it learns about form, matchups, and park effects across every market at once, instead of relearning it ten separate times on ten smaller slices of data. The model decides for itself, from the data, how much market type should shift a prediction — it isn't told the markets are different, it has to notice.

The split: per year, then pooled

Exactly as specified — not one global cutoff. Each calendar year is split 75/25 in chronological order on its own, then all four years' 75% are combined into one training set and all four years' 25% into one held-out test set.

2023
2024
2025
train (first 75% of each year, by date) test (final 25% of each year, by date)

The model never trains on a date it's later tested on, and every year contributes to both sides — so a good or bad 2024 alone can't dominate the result. All four years' test slices are then pooled into one number, which is what's reported throughout this page.

The result that got caught before it was reported

Model B's first run produced numbers far outside anything plausible elsewhere in this audit — exactly the shape of result this process has committed to stopping and investigating, not reporting.

First run — killed before reporting
+46.63%
ROI, TEST holdout · AUC 0.857 · n=2,499
↓
Corrected, after the fix
+4.98%
ROI, TEST holdout · AUC 0.764 · CI [1.13%, 8.82%]

What was actually wrong: player-prop lines are offered as a full ladder of alternates for every real game — a total-bases prop at 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, and 4.5, all at once, for the same player in the same game. The first version counted every alt-line as its own independent bet (917,981 rows collapsed to just 146,740 real player-games once corrected — a 6× inflation), and a naive fix picked the wrong line as "the" bet 90% of the time — the extreme 0.5 alt-line, not the book's real, standard line.

The fix: one row per real bet, keyed to the line whose price sits closest to a true coin flip — the book's actual primary line, not the cheapest alt-line technicality. Rerun with that fix, the impossible numbers became plausible ones, and the model's own predicted-probability-vs-actual-outcome table went from backwards (the market implying 92–97% and reality landing at 70–73%) to normal bookmaker vig (reality tracking a few points under the market's price, as expected).

7

Why "more accurate" isn't automatically "more profitable"

A real, unfabricated way to push accuracy above 80% exists in this data. It also loses money — which is exactly why it isn't what's reported as the model's headline number.

Betting whichever side the market already favors on batter_home_runs alone — no model required — wins 88.4% of the time, since most players simply don't hit a home run in a given game. ROI on that identical bet: −2.20%. The payout on such a heavy favorite is too small to cover the rare miss. Leaning a model toward that market would raise its accuracy number and lower its profitability at the same time — the two aren't the same target here, and the figure reported for Model B (68.4% accuracy, the edge>0.03 PASS) is the version that makes money rather than the version that merely looks impressive.
