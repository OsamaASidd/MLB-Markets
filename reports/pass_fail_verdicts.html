The single most honest answer to "what's the ROI on the whole data": −0.34%. That's every pick the client's entire adopted default policy would generate across all 5 markets it covers, all 4 real seasons, pooled — a confirmed small loss (CI is entirely negative, not just crossing zero). This is not in conflict with the +1.32% PASS below — that's a narrow, specific sub-segment inside one market (hits) whose own blanket ROI is flat. Selectivity, not scale, is where any value exists in this data. A validated +10% blended ROI is not achievable from this data by searching further — see Addendum 7 (an exhaustive prior search) and Addendum 15 for why.
Detail on the other two cards above, before repeating either as "confirmed." The +2.61% figure is real and honestly computed, but its 95% CI is [−5.34%, +10.55%] — it does not clear this project's own gate rule, and should be described as promising, execution-focused evidence from a small (696-pick), single-week sample, not a proven edge. The +1.32% figure is the one result in this entire audit that genuinely clears n≥500 AND CI-lower-bound > 0 on its own — CI [0.19%, 2.45%] — but it weakened when a 4th real season (2023) was added (was +1.93%, CI [0.54%, 3.33%] on 3 seasons), and a "consistent upward trend across seasons" argument used earlier to support it did not survive 2023 being added (2023 alone is flat, and the tighter −249..−150 sub-band actually reverses negative in 2023). Still the most defensible "real positive ROI" claim in this audit — just a more marginal one than first reported. Full derivation: MILESTONE_1_GATE_REPORT.md, Addendums 10, 12, 14.

06

Every strategy and model tried beyond the gate itself

Six independent custom strategies, a fine-grained odds sweep, and a real gradient-boosted model — all built on real data with proper train/test discipline, checking whether anything beats the verdicts above.

Why these are negative, specifically — not just "the market is efficient." Every row below tests something public and obvious: team records, who pitched in relief yesterday, a decade-old sabermetric stat, a park-factor table anyone can look up. A sportsbook's entire business model depends on pricing exactly these things correctly — that's not a wall unique to this audit, it's what "professionally priced market" means. A real edge here would need either information the market doesn't have yet, or a genuinely better model than what oddsmakers already run — a much higher bar than "notice something obvious first."

07

Accuracy vs. ROI — why "raise accuracy above 80%" isn't the same request as "raise ROI"

A real, unfabricated way to clear 80% accuracy exists — betting whichever side the market itself already favors, zero modeling required. It also loses money.

MarketnAccuracyROI %
batter_home_runs93,95688.4%−2.20

batter_total_bases109,65157.2%−4.19

pitcher_strikeouts10,16654.1%−4.03
spreads1,80753.5%−5.37
totals4,84648.8%−7.08

batter_home_runs alone clears 80% accuracy with no model at all — betting "under" on a home-run prop wins ~88% of the time, since most players simply don't hit a home run in a given game (matches the harness's own gate_results.csv: home_runs minus-money WR 87.9%). Skewing a pooled model toward this market — or reporting accuracy on it alone — would produce a genuine 80%+ accuracy figure right now. It would also make the model less profitable: the payout on that heavily-favored side is small enough (~+11¢ per dollar staked) that ROI on the exact same bet is −2.20%. Accuracy and profitability are different axes, and in markets like this one, pushing one pushes the other the wrong way — the same lesson Addendum 17/18 already established. Model B's reported number stays at 68.4% test accuracy / the edge>0.03 PASS, because that's the version of "accurate" that makes money. Full writeup: MILESTONE_1_GATE_REPORT.md, Addendum 24.
